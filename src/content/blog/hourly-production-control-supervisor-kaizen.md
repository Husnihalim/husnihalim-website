<p>Many Malaysian factories already have an hourly production board. The problem is that the board often becomes a reporting ritual. Numbers are written, people nod, and the line continues to drift until the target is no longer recoverable.</p>
<p>Strong hourly production control is different. It gives the supervisor a short operating rhythm: see the gap, name the loss, assign the response, and confirm whether the line is recovering before the next hour disappears.</p>

<h2>Why Hourly Production Control Breaks Down</h2>
<p>Hourly control fails when the board is treated as administration instead of a line-side management tool. Good people still struggle when the routine is weak.</p>
<ul>
  <li><strong>The review happens too late.</strong> By the time the gap is discussed, two or three hours are already gone.</li>
  <li><strong>Loss reasons are too vague.</strong> “Machine issue” or “operator slow” does not tell the team what to fix now.</li>
  <li><strong>No owner is named.</strong> Everybody hears the problem, but nobody is responsible for the next action.</li>
  <li><strong>The discussion stays at the board.</strong> If nobody goes to the actual station, the real condition stays hidden.</li>
</ul>

<h2>The Supervisor Standard Work for Hourly Review</h2>
<p>Good hourly production control should be short, visible, and repeatable. Most lines do not need a long meeting. They need the same control questions every hour.</p>

<h3>Minimum Viable Hourly Review (What to Cover)</h3>
<table>
<thead><tr><th>Review item</th><th>What “good” looks like</th><th>Common failure</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Actual vs hourly plan</td><td>Gap shown clearly by hour and cumulative trend</td><td>Only daily total is reviewed</td></tr>
<tr><td>Main loss</td><td>Exact stop, delay, hold, or bottleneck named</td><td>Generic “machine” or “manpower” label</td></tr>
<tr><td>Owner</td><td>One person responsible for the next action</td><td>Everyone is informed, no one acts</td></tr>
<tr><td>Recovery timing</td><td>Next check time and expected output recovery</td><td>No timing, only discussion</td></tr>
<tr><td>Support needed</td><td>Maintenance, quality, material, or planning escalated early</td><td>Supervisor waits too long to escalate</td></tr>
<tr><td>Visual status</td><td>Board reflects actual line condition now</td><td>Board updated after the fact</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>

<h2>The Hourly Response Checklist</h2>
<p>If you want the board to drive action, use the same response checklist every review. The goal is not to ask more questions. The goal is to get to the next action faster.</p>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Confirm the gap:</strong> Are we ahead, on plan, or behind this hour?</li>
  <li><strong>Name the obstacle:</strong> What specific point is stopping output right now?</li>
  <li><strong>Assign the next move:</strong> Who will act before the next review?</li>
  <li><strong>Escalate early:</strong> Do we need maintenance, quality, material, or planning support now?</li>
  <li><strong>Return to the line:</strong> Has the condition changed, or only the explanation changed?</li>
</ul>

<h2>How Supervisors Run the Review Without Blame</h2>
<p>Hourly production control becomes toxic when the board is used to ask who failed. That pushes operators to hide abnormalities, delay escalation, or defend themselves instead of helping recover the line.</p>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Ask what changed in the process, not who made the mistake.</strong></li>
  <li><strong>Stand at the bottleneck or abnormal point when the cause is unclear.</strong></li>
  <li><strong>Separate immediate containment from deeper root cause work.</strong></li>
  <li><strong>Use one small test at a time so the team learns what actually improves output.</strong></li>
</ul>

<h2>A Simple Diagnostic for the Supervisor Board</h2>
<p>If you are not sure whether your hourly review is really controlling production, use this quick diagnostic with your supervisors and line leaders.</p>
<table>
<thead><tr><th>Question</th><th>If the answer is yes</th><th>If the answer is no</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Can the team see the hourly gap within one minute?</td><td>The review starts from facts</td><td>The board is too slow or too messy</td></tr>
<tr><td>Is the top loss named specifically?</td><td>The team can respond faster</td><td>The same problem will be discussed again later</td></tr>
<tr><td>Does each gap have an owner and next check time?</td><td>Action is built into the routine</td><td>The review stops at discussion</td></tr>
<tr><td>Do supervisors go to the line when the gap grows?</td><td>Problems are seen at source</td><td>The board becomes office work</td></tr>
<tr><td>Are support functions pulled in early?</td><td>Recovery starts before the shift is lost</td><td>Escalation comes too late</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>
